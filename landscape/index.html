Cyber Insurance Landscape

Who sets the terms, who inside your organization is supposed to negotiate them, and where the market stands right now.

Cyber insurance stopped being a checkbox to tick somewhere around 2021, when a surge in ransomware frequency and severity produced losses that blew past what premiums had been priced for on thin historical data. The correction that followed set a pattern still shaping the market today: carriers made controls like MFA, tested offline backups, and endpoint detection a condition of coverage, and buyers who met that bar produced markedly better loss outcomes.

That improvement in insurer loss ratios, combined with more capital entering the market and carriers competing harder for well-controlled risks, has caused premiums to fall for two straight years even as attacks have grown more sophisticated.

This does not reflect declining risk. It reflects insurers being able to tell, with more confidence than they could in 2021, which buyers actually have the controls that predict fewer and cheaper claims, and pricing those buyers accordingly. Buyers who cannot demonstrate those controls are paying for the gap in exclusions, retentions, and non-renewals instead of premiums.

Making sense of that gap, who sets the terms that define it, who inside your organization is supposed to be negotiating them, and where the market sits right now, is what the rest of this section covers.

Market Structure

A cyber policy usually passes through several layers before it reaches a buyer.

Primary carriers hold the underlying risk and pay claims when a policy they've issued gets triggered. Increasingly, though, the underwriting itself, the work of evaluating a specific applicant's risk and setting the price and terms for it, is done by separate companies called managing general agents, or MGAs, who work in exchange for a share of the premium. MGAs tend to specialize narrowly in cyber, which means they are able to evaluate technical controls more precisely than a generalist carrier writing cyber as one product among many.

Behind both entities sits reinsurance, the capital that lets a carrier or MGA absorb a loss larger than its own balance sheet can take on its own. Reinsurance pricing and appetite tend to shift before primary pricing does, and carriers pass that pressure downstream with a lag of a few quarters to a year.

One structural detail to note: a policy can be written on an admitted or a surplus lines basis. Admitted carriers are licensed and rate-regulated in the buyer's state, which limits how much they can charge and how they can word a policy. Surplus lines carriers operate outside that state regulation, which gives them more flexibility on pricing and terms but less standardized oversight of what they're allowed to exclude. Surplus lines carriers now account for nearly two-thirds of all cyber insurance premium in the US, which means most buyers, likely without realizing it, are holding a policy with more negotiable but less regulated terms than the word "insurance" usually implies.

Most buyers reach this entire structure through a broker, who places the policy across multiple carriers, negotiates terms, and manages the claim process when an incident occurs. For any organization without in-house insurance expertise, the broker relationship is effectively the interface to everything above it.

Who Buys This, and How

Cyber insurance is typically a CFO-owned or risk-manager-owned purchase. The CFO or risk management team runs the process, works with the broker, and signs off on the policy. The CISO, despite being best able to understand the risks being insured, has historically functioned as an advisor, providing technical input when asked rather than driving the decision.

This division creates a predictable failure mode. Insurers routinely ask dozens of detailed questions about an organization's security controls during underwriting, and the people best equipped to answer those questions precisely are not always the people filling out the form. When the CISO is looped in late or lightly, underwriting questionnaires get answered by people without the technical grounding to answer them accurately, which produces coverage misaligned with actual risk, exclusions nobody flagged in advance, or renewal surprises when a control gap surfaces during a claim.

General counsel belongs in this conversation too, and often is included, because exclusions and breach-notification obligations are legal-exposure questions as much as technical ones. A policy's war exclusion or its definition of a covered incident is language a security team will not naturally scrutinize the way a lawyer will.

The organizations that get this right treat the purchase as a coordinated exercise between finance, security, and legal from the start, not a procurement task that technical stakeholders get pulled into after terms are mostly set.
