Coverage Components

What a cyber policy actually pays for, and why the number on the cover page tells you less than you'd think.

A cyber insurance policy is a bundle of separate coverages stitched together under one number on the cover page. That single number, the policy limit, tells you the most the policy will ever pay out. It tells you little about what happens for any specific type of loss, because most of those are capped lower by their own separate limits inside the policy. Understanding the pieces matters more than the total.

Coverage splits into two broad categories. First-party coverage pays for the buyer's own losses. Third-party coverage pays for costs the buyer faces because of someone else's action after a breach: a lawsuit from a customer or business partner, or an enforcement action from a regulator. Most confusion about "what's actually covered" comes down to not knowing which of these two buckets a given cost falls into.

First-party coverage

Breach response costs are usually the first costs a company incurs after an incident. First-party coverage pays for the immediate work: forensic investigators to figure out what happened, lawyers to guide the response, and the cost of notifying affected customers and offering them credit monitoring.

Business interruption coverage pays for income the company loses while systems are down. If a ransomware attack takes operations offline for a week, this is the coverage that replaces the revenue lost during the outage.

Cyber extortion coverage pays for ransomware response costs: the ransom payment, if the company chooses to pay one, and the cost of a negotiator to handle it.

Data restoration coverage pays to rebuild or recover data and systems damaged in an attack, separate from the business interruption caused by the outage itself.

Third-party coverage

Regulatory defense and fines coverage pays the cost of responding to a regulator's investigation after a breach, and in some cases, the fines that result, where insurance is even allowed to cover them.

Media and network liability coverage pays claims alleging that the company's own negligence let an attacker reach someone else, for example if a vendor sues because the buyer's compromised network was the entry point into the vendor's own systems.

Privacy liability coverage pays lawsuits brought by customers or partners whose data was exposed in the breach.

Where the real risk hides

The policy limit is the number everyone negotiates over. It is rarely the number that matters most when a claim actually happens.

Sublimits are smaller caps buried inside the policy that apply to specific types of loss, regardless of what the overall policy limit says. A policy with a $5 million limit might cap ransomware payments specifically at $1 million. Buyers who never read past the number on the first page often discover the sublimit only after they make a claim that exceeds the actual coverage defined on a later page. The coverage gap and sublimit visualizer shows this breakdown for your own policy numbers.

Exclusions are worse, because they don't cap coverage, they eliminate it. A war exclusion can let a carrier deny a claim entirely if an attack is attributed to a nation-state, which has become a live fight in the industry as more ransomware groups get tied to state actors. A failure-to-maintain exclusion lets a carrier deny a claim if the security controls the buyer represented as being in place, like MFA, turn out not to have actually been in place at the time of the attack. This is exactly why the underwriting requirements covered elsewhere in this section matter beyond just qualifying for a lower premium. Misrepresenting a control doesn't just risk a bad renewal. It can void the claim you bought the policy for in the first place.

The policy limit is the number that gets negotiated before a breach. The sublimits and exclusions are the numbers that all too often are only discovered during one.
