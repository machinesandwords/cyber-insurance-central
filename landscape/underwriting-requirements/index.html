Underwriting Requirements

Underwriting requirements are what a carrier wants to see before it will sell you a policy, or sell it to you at a rate that doesn't punish you for risk you don't actually carry.

The core control set

Multi-factor authentication has moved from a recommended practice to close to a baseline requirement, particularly for remote access and for any account with administrative privileges. Carriers care about this specifically because credential theft remains one of the most common ways attackers get an initial foothold, and MFA closes off a large share of that path cheaply relative to the loss it prevents. A buyer without MFA on privileged accounts should expect a higher premium, a higher retention, or an outright decline.

Endpoint detection and response tools have become similarly expected, especially for organizations of any real size. Carriers want evidence that an attacker's presence on a device will be detected and can be acted on quickly, since dwell time, how long an attacker sits undetected inside a network, correlates directly with how large and expensive a claim eventually becomes.

Tested, offline, or immutable backups matter because ransomware's leverage depends entirely on the victim having no other way to recover data. A backup that an attacker can also encrypt or delete isn't a backup carriers give much credit for. "Tested" is the operative word here too: a backup that has never actually been restored is an assumption, not a control, and some carriers now ask directly whether restoration has been tested and how recently.

A documented, and ideally rehearsed, incident response plan rounds out the core set. Carriers have seen enough claims to know that response speed and coordination materially affect how large a loss grows, and an organization with no plan tends to spend its first critical hours after an incident figuring out who's in charge instead of containing the damage.

Attestation versus evidence

For years, most of this was self-reported. A buyer filled out a questionnaire, checked boxes, and the carrier largely took the answers at face value. That's changing. A growing number of carriers now require external security scans, formal assessments, or continuous monitoring tools as a condition of coverage.

This shift matters more than it might first appear, because of how it interacts with the failure-to-maintain exclusion covered on the coverage components page. Checking a box for a control that isn't actually running is no longer just a risk of a bad renewal down the line. If a claim comes in and the carrier discovers during the investigation that a represented control wasn't real, the exclusion can void the claim entirely. Underqualifying honestly and paying a higher premium is a materially better position than misrepresenting a control and losing the claim later.

Requirements scale with size and exposure

None of this applies identically to every buyer. A small company with limited customer data and modest revenue faces a lighter underwriting bar than an enterprise handling regulated health or financial data, or one that operates infrastructure a nation-state attacker might specifically target. The core controls above are close to universal expectations at this point, but the depth of evidence a carrier demands, and how strictly it enforces the failure-to-maintain exclusion, scales with how much is actually at stake if that buyer gets breached.

Where this leaves a buyer

The organizations getting the best terms right now aren't the ones writing the most persuasive application. They're the ones who can produce evidence, not just an attestation, that the core controls are actually running, and who understand that a gap honestly disclosed is a pricing problem, while a gap concealed is a claim problem waiting to happen.
