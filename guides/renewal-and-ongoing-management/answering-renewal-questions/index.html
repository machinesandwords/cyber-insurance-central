How to Answer Cyber Insurance Renewal Questions Without Voiding Your Policy

Renewal applications have gotten longer and more specific, and not by accident. Every new question on this year's form traces back to an actual loss carriers paid out on last year. Answering imprecisely isn't a shortcut through a repetitive form. It's the same failure-to-maintain exposure covered on the coverage components page, just showing up a year earlier than a claim would surface it.

Why the questions have changed

Carriers don't add underwriting questions speculatively. They add them after a specific category of loss shows up often enough to justify asking about it directly, and three recent, well-documented incidents map directly onto three separate question categories now showing up on applications.

Questions about privileged access and remote-access authentication followed the Change Healthcare ransomware attack in February 2024. Attackers used compromised credentials to access a Citrix remote-access portal that had no multi-factor authentication enabled, and moved through the network undetected for nine days before deploying ransomware, an admission UnitedHealth's CEO confirmed in congressional testimony. The attack disrupted prescription and claims processing for providers and pharmacies across the US for weeks.

Questions about callback verification for wire transfers followed the Arup deepfake fraud in January 2024. An employee at the UK engineering firm's Hong Kong office authorized 15 separate wire transfers totaling $25.6 million after joining a video call where every other participant, including the CFO, was an AI-generated deepfake built from publicly available meeting footage. The fraud was only discovered when the employee followed up with the company's real headquarters afterward.

Questions about vendor and third-party risk sharpened after the MOVEit breach in May 2023. The Cl0p ransomware group exploited a previously unknown vulnerability in a file-transfer product used by thousands of organizations to move data between systems, not by attacking those organizations directly, but by attacking software they depended on. Over 2,700 organizations and data belonging to more than 90 million people were affected, including companies that never used MOVEit themselves but whose data passed through a vendor that did.

Strong answers versus weak answers

Take privileged access as an example, since it shows the range clearly. A strong answer describes a dedicated vaulting tool: credentials rotate automatically on use, and every session is logged. A weaker answer describes admin passwords sitting in a shared password manager with annual rotation; this usually triggers follow-up underwriting rather than an automatic decline, but it's a flag. The answer that actually produces sublimits or non-renewal is shared admin accounts that never rotate and produce no record of who used them, since that's the exact configuration behind the losses that prompted the question in the first place.

The lesson generalizes past this one example: a specific, verifiable answer reads differently to an underwriter than a vague one, even when both are technically true. "We have MFA" and "MFA is enforced on all privileged accounts, verified against our identity provider last month" are not the same answer, even if both are honest.

Callback verification: the new question not yet formalized

This is the least understood addition to recent applications, and it's cheap to close once you know it's being asked about. Callback verification means that before authorizing a wire transfer above a set threshold, commonly $10,000 or $25,000, the person authorizing it calls the recipient at a phone number that was verified independently of the payment instruction itself, not a number provided in the same email or call requesting the transfer.

Most organizations don't have this written down as an actual procedure yet, and it's the kind of thing finance teams do informally, inconsistently, or not at all. If a renewal application asks whether this control exists and the honest answer is "sort of, sometimes," that's worth fixing before the application goes in, not after. It's a policy decision and a short written procedure, not a technology purchase.

EDR versus MDR: know which you actually have

This connects directly to the endpoint detection category covered in evidence collection and packaging. Endpoint detection and response is the technology that watches device behavior and flags suspicious activity. Managed detection and response is that same technology paired with a team, in-house or outsourced, actually watching the alerts and responding to them.

The distinction matters, as evidenced by the failure pattern in Change Healthcare that resulted in nine days of undetected lateral movement inside the network. A tool that generates an alert nobody is watching produces the same outcome as no tool at all; the compromise still goes undetected for as long as it takes someone to notice. A strong answer on an application specifies both pieces: what the tool is, and who is watching it and how quickly they respond. A weaker answer states only that a platform is licensed and deployed, which is a valid control, but not as strong as having an active response capability behind it. Increasingly, carriers are asking about each separately rather than accepting one as proof of the other.

Where this leaves a buyer

If the gap between your current controls and what the application is asking for feels wide, that's not a reason to answer optimistically and hope it doesn't come up later. It's the signal covered in who should be in the room: loop in the people who can answer precisely, before the application goes in, not after a claim tests whether the answer was accurate.
