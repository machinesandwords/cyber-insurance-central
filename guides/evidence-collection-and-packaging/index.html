Evidence Collection and Packaging for Underwriters

Attestation used to be enough. A buyer would check a box confirming MFA was in place, and the questionnaire moved on. That's no longer how underwriting works. Carriers increasingly ask for proof, not just a checked box. The gap between what gets attested and what's actually true is exactly what the failure-to-maintain exclusion is written to catch. That exclusion is covered in more detail on the coverage components page.

Building that evidence package is squarely the CISO's job, per the buying-committee roles covered in who should be in the room, not a task to hand to whichever security vendor is trying to sell you a platform. This guide is a vendor-neutral walk-through of how to build the evidence package yourself, using controls you likely already have deployed.

Why a scattered evidence trail fails you

The risk of weak evidence isn't just a slower application or a slightly higher premium. It's a claim that gets denied entirely.

When a claim comes in, the carrier's investigation doesn't just look at whether an attack happened, it looks at whether the controls represented on the original application were actually in place, in the way they were described, at the time of the incident. If MFA was represented as enforced organization-wide but a legacy system was quietly excluded, or backups were represented as tested but the last actual restoration test predates the policy by two years, that gap is precisely what a failure-to-maintain exclusion is written to catch. The claim doesn't just get scrutinized more closely. It can be voided outright.

An organization with a scattered evidence trail, screenshots buried in old emails, configuration exports nobody archived, a backup test that happened once and was never repeated, isn't necessarily lying on the application. It's simply unable to prove, months or years later, that what was true at the time of underwriting is still true, or was ever fully true to begin with. A real evidence package solves that problem before it becomes one.

What a reliable evidence package contains

The specifics vary by carrier, but the core categories carriers ask about consistently are MFA, endpoint detection and response, backups, incident response planning, asset inventory, vendor and third-party risk, and security awareness training. For each, evidence means something more specific than a policy document stating the control exists.

For MFA, evidence typically means a screenshot or export of the actual identity provider configuration showing MFA enforcement, not just availability, applied across all users and, critically, all privileged accounts. The distinction between MFA being available and MFA being enforced is one of the most common gaps between what gets attested and what's actually true, since a setting that can be enabled but isn't mandatory for every account doesn't protect the organization the way a carrier assumes it does.

For endpoint detection and response, evidence means a coverage report showing what percentage of devices actually have an active agent installed and reporting, not just a statement that an EDR platform is licensed. A platform deployed to 60 percent of endpoints is a materially different risk than one deployed to 100 percent, and the coverage percentage itself is often the specific figure an underwriter wants.

For backups, evidence means documentation that backups are both offline or immutable, so they can't be encrypted or deleted alongside production data, and actually tested through a real restoration, not just scheduled. A record of when the last restoration test occurred, and what was restored, is the artifact that turns "we have backups" into something a carrier can actually credit.

For incident response, evidence means a written plan, current rather than years old, along with documentation that it's been rehearsed, typically through a tabletop exercise, with a date attached. A plan that exists as a document but has never been walked through with the people who'd actually execute it is weaker evidence than carriers increasingly expect.

For asset inventory, evidence means a current, maintained record of hardware, software, and data repositories. It is not a list assembled once during onboarding and never revisited. Carriers can't meaningfully evaluate MFA coverage, EDR deployment, or overall exposure against an environment they can't first map, and an inventory that's a year out of date undermines every other category's evidence alongside it.

For vendor and third-party risk, evidence means documentation of an actual review process: which third parties have access to systems or data and when that access was last assessed. This has moved from a formality to a real underwriting question, given how much recent breach activity traces back to compromised vendor access rather than a direct attack on the policyholder itself.

For security awareness training, evidence means dated records, not a policy statement. Carriers want to know when employees last completed training and whether a phishing simulation was run, along with the results. "We conduct annual training" without a date or a simulation outcome attached is closer to attestation than evidence, the same gap this entire guide is about closing.

Build it before you need it

The evidence package described above shouldn't get assembled for the first time during renewal week, under deadline pressure, with whoever's available pulling screenshots from memory. It should exist as a living reference, updated as controls change, so that renewal becomes a matter of confirming the package is current rather than building it from scratch.

This matters beyond speed. An evidence library maintained year-round is also the mechanism that catches drift, such as a control that was accurately represented at last renewal but has since lapsed, before a carrier or a claims investigator catches it instead.

Where this leaves a buyer

The organizations getting the best underwriting outcomes aren't necessarily the ones with the most sophisticated security stack. They're the ones who can prove, specifically and currently, that what's deployed is actually working the way it was represented. Building that proof once, and keeping it current, is a smaller task than most security teams assume, and a considerably smaller one than rebuilding trust with a carrier after a claim gets questioned.
