Who Should Be in the Room: Cyber Insurance Buying Committee Roles

Cyber insurance is typically a CFO-owned or risk-manager-owned purchase. The CFO or risk management team runs the process, works with the broker, and signs off on the policy. That ownership structure isn't unusual. Insurance has always been a finance function in most organizations. What makes cyber insurance different is that the risk being priced is almost entirely technical, and the person who owns the purchase is rarely the person who understands that risk best.

The four roles

The CFO or risk manager runs the process end to end. They own the budget, manage the broker relationship, and make the final call on which policy to buy. In a large organization, this might be a dedicated risk management function rather than the CFO personally, but the role is the same: owning the business decision of how much risk to transfer versus retain, and at what cost.

The CISO is the person best positioned to answer the questions a carrier actually asks. Underwriting questionnaires probe specific, technical details: what percentage of accounts have MFA enabled, how endpoint detection is deployed, how recently backups were tested, etc. The CISO, or their team, is usually the only group in the organization who can answer these precisely rather than approximately. Despite that, the CISO's role has historically been advisory: providing input when asked, rather than driving the purchase or reviewing the final policy language.

General counsel has a stake as well. Exclusions and breach-notification obligations are legal-exposure questions as much as technical ones. A policy's war exclusion, or its specific definition of what counts as a covered incident, is language a security team will not naturally scrutinize the way a lawyer will, and a finance team is unlikely to catch either. When general counsel is included, and often they are, it's typically for this reason: someone needs to read the policy the way they'd read any other contract the organization is bound by.

The broker is a fifth participant, even though they're not internal. A good broker does more than place the policy, they shape which questions get raised internally in the first place, translate underwriting requirements into language the buying team understands, and can flag when a carrier's terms are unusually restrictive compared to the rest of the market. A broker who only shows up at renewal time to deliver a quote is providing a fraction of what the relationship can offer.

Where the buying process breaks

The coordination gap between these roles has a predictable shape. A renewal notice arrives. The risk manager or CFO, working against a deadline, sends the underwriting questionnaire to whoever's available on the security team, not necessarily the CISO, and not necessarily someone with full visibility into every control across the environment. The questionnaire gets filled out based on what that person knows, which may be accurate for the systems they're closest to and approximate or outdated for everything else. The policy gets bound. Nobody outside finance reads the exclusions closely, because nobody flagged that as a step.

Six months later, an incident happens. During the claims investigation, the carrier finds that a control represented as active on the questionnaire, such as MFA on a specific system or tested backups for a specific environment, wasn't actually in place, or wasn't in place the way it was described. This is precisely the scenario the failure-to-maintain exclusion exists for, and it can void the claim entirely. The organization discovers, at the worst possible moment, that the gap between what was claimed and what was true had been sitting there since the questionnaire was filled out, unreviewed by the one person who would have caught it.

What coordination actually looks like

None of this requires a complicated process. It requires a few specific handoffs happening at the right point in the renewal timeline, rather than not happening at all.

The CISO, or a senior member of their team with genuine cross-environment visibility, should review the underwriting questionnaire before it's submitted, not after. This is a factual accuracy check, not a negotiation; the goal is making sure what's represented to the carrier is true.

General counsel should read the actual exclusions and the incident definition before the policy is signed, not just the coverage summary. Brokers can usually flag which sections warrant this kind of read, and this is a reasonable request.

The CFO or risk manager should treat the CISO and general counsel's sign-off as a gate in the renewal timeline, not an optional courtesy extended if time allows. Building this in early, at the start of the renewal window rather than the week before it closes, is what actually makes the coordination possible instead of aspirational.

Where this leaves a buyer

The cost of skipping this coordination isn't visible at purchase. The premium gets paid, the policy gets filed, and everything looks fine until a claim exposes exactly what wasn't checked. By then, the fix isn't available.

The organizations that get this right treat the buying committee as a real committee, with each role doing the specific check only they're positioned to do, before the policy is signed rather than after something goes wrong.
