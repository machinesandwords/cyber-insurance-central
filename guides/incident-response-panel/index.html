The Incident Response Panel: Why Your Policy May Already Choose Your Vendors

Most organizations assume that when an incident happens, they'll call their own IT provider or a forensics firm they already trust. For many cyber policies, that assumption is wrong, and finding out during an active incident is an expensive way to learn it.

Why this exists, and what it costs to ignore

Most carriers maintain a panel of pre-approved vendors, forensic investigators, breach counsel, and sometimes communications firms, that policyholders are expected to use when an incident occurs. The logic from the carrier's side is straightforward: these vendors have already been vetted, often work at negotiated rates, and the carrier has a working relationship with them that speeds up the response.

The cost of not knowing this falls entirely on the policyholder. Engaging an outside vendor, even a highly competent one, without carrier approval can result in those specific costs being excluded from the claim. This isn't a minor administrative wrinkle. An organization that calls its own trusted IT provider first, the natural instinct in a crisis, can end up paying those costs out of pocket even though it has a policy that would have covered a panel vendor doing the same work.

Panel requirements aren't uniformly rigid across every carrier. Some frame panel use as effectively mandatory. Others present it as strongly incentivized rather than absolute, offering faster response and coordination through the panel without technically prohibiting outside vendors, though even in that case, using an unapproved vendor typically still requires carrier notification and carries more coverage risk than using the panel. The specific terms vary enough by carrier and policy that this is worth confirming directly rather than assuming either way.

The move most buyers miss: pre-approve your own vendors

Here is the part almost nobody knows to ask about. If an organization already has a trusted relationship, a forensics firm that knows the environment, or a law firm with relevant sector experience, several carriers have a process for adding that vendor to the policy as an approved non-panel provider. Chubb, for example, allows exactly this: a policyholder can request that a specific outside provider be added to the policy as an approved provider, but only if it happens before an incident or claim occurs. Once something is actively underway, that door closes.

This means the decision about which vendors will respond to an incident isn't something to leave until the incident happens. It's a decision available at purchase or renewal, and it disappears the moment it's actually needed.

This is a contract-review task, not a crisis-discovery task

Reviewing panel requirements and requesting any preferred vendor additions is exactly the kind of policy detail general counsel should be checking during the purchase or renewal process, per who should be in the room. It sits alongside exclusions and breach-notification language as the kind of contract detail a security team won't naturally think to scrutinize and a finance team isn't positioned to catch.

Once panel vendors are confirmed, whether that's the carrier's default panel or a pre-approved addition, their contact information belongs in the same place as the rest of the incident response plan covered in evidence collection and packaging: current, accessible, and part of the living document a team actually uses when an incident happens, not a detail buried in a policy PDF nobody's opened since it was signed.

Where this leaves a buyer

Organizations that avoid this problem aren't the ones with the best vendor relationships. They're the ones who confirmed, before anything went wrong, whether those relationships were actually usable under the policy they were paying for.
