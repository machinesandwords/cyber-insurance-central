How to Compare Cyber Insurance Quotes: A CISO's Guide

Most guides on comparing cyber insurance quotes are written for a generalist buyer or a broker: build a grid, match the sublimits, read the exclusions, don't buy on price alone. That advice is fine as far as it goes. It's also not written for the person in the room who actually knows whether the controls being represented on each application are real. This guide is.

The quote isn't just about the carrier

Two organizations with genuinely identical risk profiles can receive different quotes from the same carrier. The reason often has nothing to do with their security posture. It has to do with how the application was presented, and which markets the broker actually submitted it to.

A skilled broker frames an application to highlight the controls a specific underwriter weighs most heavily, submits to carriers whose appetite actually matches the buyer's risk profile, and knows which underwriters will engage in a real conversation about a borderline control versus which will simply decline. A less skilled or less engaged broker submits the same raw application everywhere and lets the numbers speak for themselves, which they often do poorly, regardless of how strong the underlying environment actually is.

This matters directly to a CISO comparing quotes. If your organization's quote looks meaningfully worse than what a peer company reports, the first diagnostic question isn't necessarily "is our security worse." It might be "was our submission weaker." That's a broker conversation, not a controls conversation, and confusing the two leads a security team to chase control improvements that were never the actual problem.

Not all carriers are evaluating you the same way

Quotes also differ because carriers themselves differ in how they underwrite, not just in what they charge. A traditional, generalist carrier writing cyber as one line among several tends to lean on a standard questionnaire and a fairly conventional read of your controls. A tech-driven MGA, the kind covered on the carriers page, often rewards specific, evidenced controls more precisely, sometimes because it has direct tooling or monitoring integration that lets it see more than a questionnaire response.

The practical effect: an organization with genuinely strong, well-instrumented security might see its most favorable quote from an MGA built to recognize that maturity, while a generalist carrier prices the same environment more conservatively simply because its underwriting process wasn't built to detect the difference. A CISO comparing quotes side by side should read a lower quote from an unfamiliar name not as automatically suspicious, but as potentially a signal that a more technically sophisticated underwriter correctly priced a strong environment, rather than a red flag to dismiss.

What to actually check, once quotes are in hand

Once quotes exist side by side, the standard comparison points still matter.

Confirm the sublimits on the loss types most likely to actually generate a claim, ransomware and business interruption in particular, match what was discussed verbally. Brokers sometimes reference a sublimit during the sales conversation that turns out to be lower once you read the bound policy's actual language, and that gap must be caught before signing, not after a claim comes in under what you expected.

Check that the exclusion language, particularly the failure-to-maintain exclusion, matches what was actually represented in the underwriting questionnaire. If the questionnaire attested to a control that isn't fully deployed everywhere it implied, that gap is worth resolving before signing, not discovering during a claim.

Confirm whether each quote is a standalone cyber policy or an endorsement added to a broader liability policy. Endorsements typically carry lower limits and narrower triggers, and comparing an endorsement's premium against a standalone policy's premium isn't a real comparison at all.

Where this leaves a buyer

The comparison a CISO is uniquely positioned to make isn't premium against premium. It's whether each quote's underlying assumptions about the environment are actually accurate, and whether a quote that looks worse reflects real risk or a weaker submission. Nobody else on the buying committee, per the roles covered in who should be in the room, is positioned to make that specific call.
